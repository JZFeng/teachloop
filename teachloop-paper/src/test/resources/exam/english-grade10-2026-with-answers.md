**C**
My studies once showed that students do better when they take notes by hand than when they use laptops. Before I knew it, those studies were used as good reasons for laptop bans in classrooms in the U.S. I received a lot of emails blaming me for such ideas. The fact is that I don't actually support laptop bans, and never have. In fact, I don't even ban laptops in my own classes.

In the research, we asked students to watch TED Talks and had them take notes either on laptops or with a pen and paper. Students using laptops recorded the talks nearly with every word, while students who took handwritten notes could not; they just took notes in their own words. Writing by hand required students to understand and summarize the talk. And deeper processing of the information led to improved learning. As a result, students in this case get higher points on tests.

My findings came out. Teachers around the country soon started banning laptops in their classes. But they didn't know what actually made learning difficult was the mindless recording of a lecture. Laptops just let students take notes more quickly.

Like most technology, laptops are not universally helpful or harmful in class. Whether they are helpful or harmful depends on the goal. The value of a laptop in the classroom depends on the goal of a lesson plan, the nature of the text, the kind of students being taught, etc.

So can laptops be used in the classroom? If a lesson requires exact quotes, then laptops are excellent. But if the goal is conceptual understanding, they will produce the exactly opposite results. Don't forget the situation when deciding whether or not to allow laptops in your class. All in all, the answer to the question is that it depends.

**D**
The transition from junior to senior high school is a significant milestone for teenagers worldwide. In China, this period, often referred to as "Gaoyi," marks not only an increase in academic rigor but also a critical stage of personal growth and identity formation. New subjects, more complex concepts, and higher expectations from teachers and parents can create much stress. However, it is this challenge that fosters resilience and independence.

One of the most noticeable changes is the teaching style. Unlike junior high, where teachers often spoon-feed knowledge, senior high teachers tend to encourage more independent thinking and self-directed learning. Students are expected to preview lessons, actively participate in discussions, and complete extended reading tasks. This change requires them to develop better organizational skills and time management strategies.

Socially, Gaoyi students are also forming new relationships. They may be in classes with peers from different junior high schools, necessitating the building of new friendships. Extra-curricular activities, such as clubs and sports teams, provide valuable opportunities for social integration and the exploration of interests beyond academics. These experiences lead to a well-developed personality, which is highly valued in the holistic education method supported by many modern high schools.

Psychologically, the stress to do well academically, especially with the National College Entrance Examination (Gaokao) in three years, can be intense. It is important for students to develop healthy coping mechanisms, such as regular exercise, hobbies, and open communication with trusted adults. Schools are increasingly aware of this and are offering more services and mental health workshops to support student well-being.

Finally, Gaoyi is more than just preparation for university; it is a foundational year for developing lifelong learning habits, emotional intelligence, and a clearer sense of self. Facing the challenges with a positive attitude can turn this stressful period into a benefit journey of growth.
